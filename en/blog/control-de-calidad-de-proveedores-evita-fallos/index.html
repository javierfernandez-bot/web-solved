Supplier Quality Control: Avoid Failures

In the food industry, a supply chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Implementing robust supplier quality control isn't optional — it's a necessity for any company that wants to guarantee food safety, comply with current regulations, and protect its reputation. A single failure in raw materials can compromise the entire production process, trigger costly product recalls, and, more seriously, put the end consumer's health at risk. In this article, we explain how to structure an effective system for evaluating and controlling your suppliers, from first contact through ongoing deliveries.

Why Control Your Suppliers' Quality

The most obvious reason is traceability and legal liability. As set out in Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, laying down the general principles of food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are responsible for ensuring that the food and feed they sell meets the relevant safety requirements. That directly includes everything they source from third parties.

Beyond regulatory compliance, controlling supplier quality delivers concrete operational benefits:

  • Fewer non-conformities: catching deviations at the source stops the problem from ever reaching the production line.
  • Cost savings: fewer returns, less waste, and fewer unplanned production stoppages.
  • Better supplier-customer relationships: when a supplier knows your criteria, they work to meet them, leading to smoother, more transparent collaboration.
  • Certification audit readiness: schemes like BRCGS (BRCGS, n.d.) or IFS require documented evidence of the control exercised over the supply chain.

In short, controlling your suppliers means protecting your product, your brand, and your consumers.

Approval Criteria and Ongoing Evaluation

Supplier approval is the starting point for any quality management system in the supply chain. It means confirming, before starting a business relationship, that the supplier meets a set of minimum requirements defined by your company. These criteria are usually grouped into several categories:

  • Legal and technical documentation: health registrations, certificates of analysis, product data sheets, allergen declarations, and liability insurance.
  • Quality certifications: checking whether a supplier holds recognized certifications like ISO 22000, BRCGS, or IFS simplifies the initial assessment and reduces your own verification burden.
  • Track record and references: industry experience, references from other customers, and years in operation are all indicators of operational maturity.
  • Incident response capability: how does the supplier handle a non-conformity? Do they have traceability and recall protocols in place?

Once a supplier is approved, the evaluation doesn't stop there. Ongoing evaluation is essential for maintaining quality over time. The most common metrics for assessing a supplier's periodic performance include the non-conformity rate per batch received, on-time delivery, the quality of documentation provided, and how quickly issues are resolved.

We recommend setting an evaluation frequency (quarterly, biannual, or annual) based on purchase volume and the risk associated with the raw material or service supplied. Critical suppliers — those providing ingredients with high microbiological risk or allergens — should be reviewed more often.

Supplier Audits, Step by Step

Supplier audits are the most powerful tool for verifying, on site, that a supplier's practices match what's stated in their documents. Running them in a structured way maximizes their value and minimizes the time invested. Here's the process in four phases:

1. Planning

Define the audit's scope: will you review only facility hygiene, or also the HACCP system, allergen management, and pest control? Build a checklist based on your company's requirements and the applicable regulations. Give the supplier enough advance notice of the date and scope so they can prepare the necessary documentation.

2. Execution

During the visit, combine document review with direct observation of the facilities and processes. Systematically log all evidence — photos, copies of records, staff interviews. A digital checklist makes this task easier and eliminates the risk of losing information.

3. Report and Scoring

After the audit, prepare a detailed report of the deviations found, classified by severity (critical, major, minor). Assign an overall score that lets you compare suppliers against each other and track them over time.

4. Following Up on Corrective Actions

Every deviation should come with a corrective action plan that has an owner and a deadline. Verify that the actions have actually been implemented before closing out the audit. Without this follow-up, the whole exercise loses much of its value.
