Food Safety: The Essential Guide

Food safety is one of the fundamental pillars of any food industry company. Guaranteeing that the food reaching consumers is safe, unadulterated and fit for consumption isn't just a legal obligation — it's an ethical responsibility and a key competitive factor. In this article you'll find a practical, complete guide to understanding what food safety involves, which hazards you need to control, what management systems exist, and how digitization can take your controls to the next level.

What food safety is, and why it's critical

When we talk about food safety in the context of the food and beverage industry, we mean the guarantee that a food will not cause harm to the consumer when prepared and consumed as intended. This concept is closely tied to the idea of food hygiene, and spans everything from primary production to distribution and final consumption.

The importance of food safety can't be overstated. A control failure can trigger health alerts, product recalls, financial penalties and, above all, irreparable harm to consumers' health and to the company's reputation. As set out in Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, laying down the general principles of food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are responsible for ensuring that the food they sell is safe, and must be able to trace any product throughout the entire food chain.

Effective food safety management protects people, strengthens customer trust and opens the door to domestic and international markets. In an increasingly demanding regulatory environment, quality managers need up-to-date tools and knowledge to meet the sector's challenges. Physical, chemical and biological hazards

The first step in managing food safety is identifying and classifying the hazards that can compromise a food's safety. Traditionally, these fall into three broad categories:

Biological hazards: The most common, and the ones that pose the greatest risk to public health. They include pathogenic bacteria such as Salmonella, Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli or Campylobacter, as well as viruses, parasites and mycotoxin-producing fungi. Biological contamination can occur at any point in the chain, from raw material to finished product, and is controlled through hygiene practices, proper temperatures and periodic microbiological testing.

Chemical hazards: These include pesticide residues, heavy metals, unauthorized additives, undeclared allergens, cleaning and disinfection products, or contaminants formed during processing (such as acrylamide or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons). Supplier control, proper allergen management and chemical testing are the main barriers against this type of hazard.

Physical hazards: Fragments of glass, metal, wood, plastic, bone or stone that can accidentally end up in food during production or handling. Metal detection systems, filters and visual inspections are essential tools for minimizing this risk.

Systematically identifying, assessing and controlling these hazards is the foundation any robust food safety management system is built on. A genuine food safety culture within the organization is equally decisive: written procedures aren't enough if the team doesn't understand them, buy into them and apply them day to day.

Management systems: HACCP and ISO 22000

Once hazards are identified, you need a structured system to manage them. The two major reference frameworks are HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) and the ISO 22000 standard.

HACCP is a preventive methodology, internationally recognized and required by European law, that lets you identify, assess and control significant food safety hazards. It's based on seven core principles: hazard analysis, determination of Critical Control Points (CCPs), establishment of critical limits, a monitoring system, corrective measures, verification procedures, and a documentation system. Implementing it is mandatory for most food businesses, and it forms the core of any food safety management system.

The ISO 22000 standard takes it a step further: it integrates HACCP principles with the requirements of a management system, following the high-level structure shared with other standards like ISO 9001. This lets organizations manage food safety in an integrated way alongside other quality management systems. According to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO, n.d.), ISO 22000 applies to any organization in the food chain, regardless of size or complexity, and provides an internationally recognized framework for demonstrating a commitment to food safety.

Alongside ISO 22000, there are other widely recognized certification schemes in the sector, such as BRCGS, IFS or FSSC 22000, which add further requirements and are frequently required by major distributors and retail chains. Choosing the right scheme depends on the markets your company operates in and the requirements of your customers.

Implementing and maintaining these systems requires significant investment in training, documentation and audits. But the benefits are clear: reduced risk, tighter process control, access to new markets, and a solid foundation for continuous improvement. Building a genuine food safety culture — where everyone on the team, from production to management, understands and commits to food safety — matters just as much as any technical procedure.
