Documenting Issues with Digital Evidence

By Karim · October 10, 2025 · 6 min read

The need to document issues digitally on the plant floor has never been more urgent. Factories that still rely on paper records, printed spreadsheets or handwritten notes face a constant risk: documents that go missing, incomplete records, information that takes too long to reach the people responsible, and delays during audits or certifications. In an industrial environment where every minute counts, that approach is no longer good enough.

Digital evidence — photos, videos, audio recordings or attached documents — offers a practical, powerful alternative. It lets you capture what's happening in real time, brings traceability to your processes, and ensures information flows instantly between teams. In this article we'll look at what digital evidence actually is, the benefits of adopting it, best practices for using it on the floor, how to integrate it with your existing systems, and how tools like Solved simplify the whole process.

What digital evidence means for issues

To understand why it's so important to document issues digitally, it helps to define what we mean by evidence in an industrial setting. It's objective proof attached to the record of an issue, non-conformity or other relevant event on the factory floor. This evidence confirms what happened and makes both analysis and resolution easier.

The most common types include:

  • Photos: images showing a fault on a production line, a defect in a product, or an anomaly in a piece of equipment.
  • Videos: recordings that let you observe processes in motion, recurring faults, or behavior that's hard to describe in words.
  • Audio: useful for capturing quick observations or operator statements without having to write anything down.
  • Attached technical documents: drawings, maintenance reports, lab results, or product spec sheets.

The key point is that this digital evidence delivers traceability. It doesn't just record that an issue happened — it captures how it looked, who detected it, and what proof is available for follow-up. For ISO, IFS or BRC audits, that level of detail is often what separates passing an inspection from failing one.

Benefits of documenting issues digitally

Adopting a system to document issues digitally brings clear advantages that easily outweigh the initial investment.

  • Greater data accuracy: evidence removes ambiguity. A photo of a defective batch or a video of a machine failing carries far more value than a brief written description.
  • Faster communication: issues reach the responsible person instantly, with every detail attached. This cuts response times and prevents confusion.
  • Easier audits: auditors can review records backed by objective proof. This saves hours otherwise spent hunting for paperwork or unclear explanations.
  • Evidence-based decisions: a quality manager can make decisions based on verifiable visual information instead of relying on subjective interpretation.

Centralizing digital evidence also makes it possible to analyze it over time, spot patterns and prevent recurrences.

Best practices for capturing evidence on the floor

Simply having the option to document issues digitally isn't enough — you need good practices in place so the information captured is genuinely useful.

  1. Use mobile devices and tablets
     Let operators and supervisors log issues directly on the floor from their phone or tablet. This guarantees immediacy and removes the need to transfer information into another system later.
  2. Always attach context
     Every piece of evidence should come with basic information: date and time, location, production line, responsible person and issue category. A photo with no context loses its value in an audit.
  3. Keep formats consistent
     Define standards for photo and video quality, file naming, and the types of documents that can be attached. This makes everything easier to find later.
  4. Secure, accessible storage
     Evidence should be stored in the cloud or on a central server with controlled access permissions. This prevents loss and guarantees availability even during external audits.

These practices ensure that documenting issues digitally becomes an efficient task that delivers real value to the quality team.

Integrating digital evidence with existing systems

A key factor in maximizing the value of documenting issues digitally is integration with the factory's other systems. Evidence shouldn't stay siloed in a single piece of software — it needs to flow to the systems that need it.

  • With ERP: issues can be linked to batches, orders or customers, creating a dynamic, coherent database.
  • With MES: faults detected in production get tied to specific work orders or lines.
  • With CMMS: evidence is used to justify maintenance interventions and log repairs.

Centralizing all of this information prevents duplication, ensures traceability across departments, and simplifies audit preparation.

Common challenges and how to solve them

Although the benefits are clear, many factories run into obstacles when implementing the practice of documenting issues digitally.

  • Connectivity problems: WiFi or data coverage isn't always stable on some plants. The fix is to use apps that work offline and sync afterward.
  • Large files: high-resolution videos or photos take up space. Automatic compression or per-file size limits are recommended.
  • Team resistance: some operators may see digital logging as extra work. The key is simple training and showing the direct benefits.
  • Data privacy: photos or videos can show sensitive information. Clear access permissions and policies are essential.

Overcoming these challenges ensures a smooth transition to digitization.
