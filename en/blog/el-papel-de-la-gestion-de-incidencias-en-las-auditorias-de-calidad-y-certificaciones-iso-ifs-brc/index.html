In today's industrial environment, where excellence and regulatory compliance are essential requirements for staying competitive, quality audits and international certifications such as ISO, IFS or BRC are an essential part of operations. These standards not only guarantee product quality, but also consistency, traceability and continuous improvement within the organization.

However, many plants still face the same obstacle during audits: poor incident management, scattered or manual. Auditors ask for specific evidence, and teams lose hours searching for records, filling out paperwork or trying to justify corrective actions that went undocumented.

In this article we'll look at how incident management becomes a strategic tool for passing audits and certifications such as ISO 9001, IFS Food or BRCGS, the benefits of having a robust digital system, the most common mistakes made during audits and how a platform like Solved simplifies the whole process.

The relationship between incidents and quality audits

Every quality audit — whether internal or external — has a common goal: verifying that processes are under control, that non-conformities are properly managed and that there is full traceability of incidents.

Auditors don't focus solely on final results, but on the process of detecting, recording, analyzing and resolving incidents. Good management shows that the organization responds to problems, analyzes them and prevents them from recurring, meeting the continuous improvement principle required by ISO, IFS and BRC standards.

For example, during an audit, an auditor may ask for:

  • Evidence of the record of a specific incident and its corrective actions.
  • Detection, follow-up and closure dates.
  • Identification of the owner.
  • Confirmation that the action taken prevented recurrence.

If this data is presented clearly and digitally, the audit flows easily. But if records are scattered, incomplete or on paper, the process becomes slow and generates observations.

Benefits of robust incident management for audits

Having a solid system for incident management in quality audits and ISO, IFS or BRC certifications brings significant advantages in both operational efficiency and documentary compliance.

1. Full traceability of every incident
 Traceability is one of the fundamental pillars of any quality standard. With digital incident management, every event is recorded with date, time, owner and associated evidence. This makes it possible to show the auditor that processes are controlled and verifiable.

2. Full history with evidence
 A digital system makes it possible to attach photos, videos, lab reports or meeting minutes directly to each incident. This way, all the information is centralized and accessible from a single place. This removes the need to search through physical folders or old emails during the audit.

3. Less time spent on document preparation
 Companies that manage incidents manually can take days to prepare for an audit. With specialized software, reports are generated automatically and presented in seconds. This way, the audit stops being a stressful process and becomes a simple review of evidence.

4. Continuous improvement and non-conformity prevention
 Robust incident management not only helps pass audits, but also improves internal quality. By analyzing root causes and applying effective corrective actions, the organization reduces errors and shows a real commitment to continuous improvement.

5. Simplified compliance with multiple standards
 A digital system makes it possible to structure incident records in line with the requirements of different standards (ISO 9001, ISO 22000, IFS, BRCGS), avoiding duplication and ensuring consistency in the information presented to auditors or customers.

How to connect incident management with ISO, IFS and BRC standards

Each standard has its own requirements, but they all agree on one point: proper management of incidents and corrective actions is essential to obtain and maintain certification.

Let's look at how this applies to the main certifications:

ISO 9001 – Quality Management System

The ISO 9001 standard requires the organization to identify, control and analyze non-conformities, in addition to demonstrating the effectiveness of the actions taken to prevent recurrence.

Digital incident management makes it possible to:

  • Record every non-conformity in detail.
  • Assign owners and follow-up dates.
  • Attach evidence and verifications.
  • Generate automatic reports for internal and external audits.

With this traceability, the auditor can review the complete cycle of an incident: detection, root cause analysis, corrective action and verification of the outcome.

IFS Food – Food safety and product quality

IFS Food requires a documented system that ensures full traceability of incidents and the implementation of fast corrective actions.

Good incident management in this context makes it possible to:

  • Document failures in raw materials, production or storage.
  • Link automatic alerts to critical incidents.
  • Verify compliance with correction due dates.
  • Ensure incidents are resolved before they affect the final product.

In addition, IFS auditors look favorably on companies that have centralized digital systems, as this reflects control, efficiency and commitment to food safety.

BRCGS – Global standards for food safety

The BRCGS standard places special emphasis on preventing non-conformities and documenting all corrective and preventive actions (CAPA).

Common challenges in quality audits

Although international standards set clear guidelines, many plants keep making the same mistakes during audits. Most stem from a manual, decentralized, or inefficient incident management.

1. Incomplete or outdated manual documentation
 Using spreadsheets, paper forms, or scattered emails causes information loss. During the audit, auditors find incomplete or inconsistent records.

2. Errors in records
 Incorrect dates, wrongly assigned owners, or ambiguous descriptions raise doubts about the reliability of the system. This often leads to findings or the need for additional audits.

3. Lack of follow-up on corrective actions
 Many incidents get recorded but never properly closed. Auditors find open actions or actions with no verification evidence, which affects the final rating.

4. Lack of cross-departmental traceability
 When each department (quality, production, maintenance) manages its incidents separately, there's no overall view of the system. This makes it harder to demonstrate control and consistency during the audit.
