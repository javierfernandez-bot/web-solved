Quality Control in Canned Goods: Challenges and Digital Solutions

The quality control in canned goods is one of the most demanding processes within the food industry. Canning companies work with products that must guarantee safety and stability for months or even years, which means a chain of rigorous checks from the receipt of raw materials to the dispatch of the finished product. In an increasingly regulated and competitive environment, digitalizing these processes is no longer an option: it's a strategic necessity.

Main quality challenges in the canning industry

Companies in the canning sector face specific challenges that set them apart from other food industries. The nature of the product —subjected to intense heat treatments and stored for long periods— demands thorough control at every stage of the production process. These are the most common challenges we identify in canning companies across Spain:

Managing heat treatments and sterilization. Controlling sterilization or pasteurization is the critical point par excellence for quality in the canning industry. Any deviation in temperature, time or pressure can compromise the safety of the final product. Recording, validating and archiving this data manually is slow, error-prone and hard to audit.

Traceability across the entire chain. As set out in the Regulation (EC) 178/2002 on general food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food operators are required to guarantee the traceability of their products at every stage of production, processing and distribution. For a canning company handling multiple batches, suppliers and product references, building this traceability manually takes enormous effort and poses a real risk in the event of a food safety alert.

Container and seal control. Container integrity is essential in canned goods. A faulty seal can lead to microbiological contamination, loss of vacuum or product spoilage. Double-seam, tightness and seaming checks must be carried out frequently and properly documented.

Document management and regulatory compliance. Procedures, HACCP records, cleaning plans, equipment calibrations, supplier certificates… The documentation load of a canning company is enormous. Keeping everything up to date, accessible and organized for an audit is one of the biggest sources of strain for quality managers.

Staff turnover and ongoing training. Seasonal campaigns bring mass hiring. Ensuring that every operator knows and correctly applies quality procedures is an operational challenge that directly affects product consistency.

Which critical checks can't fail in the canning sector

Beyond the general challenges, there are checks whose omission or failure can have serious consequences, both for consumer safety and business continuity. A solid quality management system must guarantee the correct execution and recording of the following checks:

HACCP and critical control points (CCPs). Hazard analysis and critical control points is the backbone of food safety in canned goods. As set out in the Regulation (EC) 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs (Regulation [EC] 852/2004, 2004), all food business operators must put in place, apply and maintain procedures based on HACCP principles. In a canning company, the CCPs usually include heat treatment, pH control, water activity and seal inspection.

Control of raw materials and suppliers. The quality of the final product largely depends on the quality of the raw materials. Verifying specifications, reviewing certificates and carrying out incoming checks is essential to prevent a problem at source from reaching the end consumer.

Calibration and maintenance of critical equipment. Autoclaves, thermometers, pressure gauges, double-seaming equipment… The reliability of check results depends directly on the condition and calibration of the measuring instruments. A miscalibrated piece of equipment can invalidate hours of production and compromise the safety of the batch.

Microbiological and physicochemical control of the finished product. Finished product analyses make it possible to validate that the treatment was effective and that the product meets the established safety parameters.

Management of non-conformities and corrective actions. Detecting a deviation is only the first step. Recording it correctly, investigating its root cause and implementing an effective corrective action is what sets apart a company that learns from its mistakes from one that repeats them.
