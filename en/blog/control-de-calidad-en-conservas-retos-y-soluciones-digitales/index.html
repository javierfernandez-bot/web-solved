Quality control in canning: challenges and digital solutions

Quality control in canning is one of the most demanding processes in the food industry. Canning companies work with products that must remain safe and stable for months or even years, which requires a chain of rigorous controls from raw material receiving through to shipping the finished product. In an increasingly regulated and competitive environment, digitizing these processes is no longer optional — it's a strategic necessity.

Main quality challenges in the canning industry

Canning companies face specific challenges that set them apart from other food industries. The nature of the product — subjected to intense heat treatment and stored for long periods — demands thorough control at every stage of production. These are the most common challenges we see in canning companies across the country:

Managing heat treatment and sterilization. Controlling sterilization or pasteurization is the critical point above all others for quality in the canning industry. Any deviation in temperature, time or pressure can compromise the safety of the final product. Recording, validating and filing this data manually is slow, error-prone and hard to audit.

Traceability across the entire chain. As set out in Regulation (EC) 178/2002 on general food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are required to ensure the traceability of their products at every stage of production, processing and distribution. For a canning company handling multiple batches, suppliers and product references, building this traceability manually takes enormous effort and carries real risk in the event of a food safety alert.

Packaging and seal control. Container integrity is essential in canning. A faulty seal can lead to microbiological contamination, loss of vacuum or product spoilage. Double-seam and seal-integrity checks need to be run frequently and properly documented.

Document management and regulatory compliance. Procedures, HACCP records, cleaning plans, equipment calibrations, supplier certificates... the documentation load at a canning company is enormous. Keeping everything up to date, accessible and organized for an audit is one of the biggest pain points for quality managers.

Staff turnover and ongoing training. Seasonal campaigns mean bringing on large numbers of new staff at once. Making sure every operator knows and correctly applies quality procedures is an operational challenge that directly affects product consistency.

Critical controls that can't fail in the canning industry

Beyond the general challenges, some controls carry serious consequences if they're skipped or fail — both for consumer safety and for business continuity. A solid quality management system must ensure the following controls are properly carried out and recorded:

HACCP and critical control points (CCPs). Hazard analysis and critical control points form the backbone of food safety in canning. As set out in Regulation (EC) 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs (Regulation [EC] 852/2004, 2004), every food business operator must put in place, implement and maintain procedures based on HACCP principles. At a canning company, CCPs typically include heat treatment, pH control, water activity and seal inspection.

Raw material and supplier control. The quality of the final product depends heavily on the quality of the raw materials. Verifying specifications, reviewing certificates and running receiving checks is essential to stop a problem at the source from reaching the end consumer.

Calibration and maintenance of critical equipment. Autoclaves, thermometers, pressure gauges, double-seaming equipment... the reliability of control results depends directly on the condition and calibration of measuring instruments. A miscalibrated piece of equipment can invalidate hours of production and put a batch's safety at risk.

Microbiological and physicochemical control of the finished product. Finished-product testing confirms that the treatment was effective and that the product meets the established safety parameters.

Non-conformity management and corrective actions. Detecting a deviation is only the first step. Recording it correctly, investigating its root cause and implementing an effective corrective action is what separates a company that learns from its mistakes from one that repeats them.
