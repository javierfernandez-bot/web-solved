Food quality standards and regulations

By Claudia · October 31, 2025 · 6 min read

Food quality standards are the framework that guarantees safety, traceability and consumer trust in the products that reach the market. In a sector as sensitive as food, where a single failure can have serious consequences for public health and a company's reputation, holding certifications and meeting these standards isn't optional — it's a strategic obligation.

In this article we'll look at what food industry quality standards are, which are the most relevant internationally, what benefits they bring to factories, how to prepare to meet them, and how digital tools like Solved make traceability and audits easier.

What are food quality standards?

Food quality standards are regulations, protocols and certifications that set out the requirements a food company must meet to guarantee the safety of its products. They cover everything from raw material traceability to production, storage, transport and distribution processes.

These standards aim to:

  • Guarantee consumer safety.
  • Ensure compliance with national and international regulations.
  • Demonstrate transparency and traceability across the whole chain.
  • Prevent health risks and mass product recalls.
  • Strengthen the trust of customers, distributors and authorities.

In practice, meeting food quality standards means a factory has solid control systems, thorough documentation and processes geared toward continuous improvement.

Key quality standards in the food industry

There are many norms and certifications out there, but a handful are recognized globally and required by major distribution chains.

ISO 22000: food safety management

  • What it is: an international standard that integrates the principles of the HACCP system (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points).
  • What it requires: batch traceability, risk control at every stage of the process, and documented procedures.
  • Practical example: a dairy plant must log pasteurization temperatures and milk traceability to guarantee food safety.

IFS (International Featured Standards)

  • What it is: a standard heavily required in Europe, especially by distribution chains.
  • What it requires: full backward (suppliers) and forward (customers) traceability, food safety controls, packaging management and periodic audits.
  • Practical example: a spice supplier must be able to show which batch its raw materials came from and which finished products they were used in.

BRCGS (Brand Reputation Compliance Global Standard)

  • What it is: a British standard recognized worldwide.
  • What it requires: exhaustive traceability, supplier control, product recall plans, and unannounced audits.
  • Practical example: a juice plant must be able to show which farm supplied the fruit and which supermarkets each bottle reached.

ISO 9001 applied to the food industry

  • What it is: a quality management standard that applies across all sectors.
  • What it requires: process documentation, continuous improvement, customer satisfaction.
  • Practical example: a canned-goods manufacturer that wants to demonstrate efficiency and quality across its whole production process.

ISO 14001 in the food industry

  • What it is: an environmental management standard applicable to food factories.
  • What it requires: control of discharges, waste, emissions and energy consumption.
  • Practical example: a meat-processing plant that needs to demonstrate responsible management of organic and plastic waste.

These are the main food quality standards every company should consider if it wants to compete in demanding markets and reach major distributors.

Benefits of meeting food quality standards

Implementing these standards isn't just about ticking a legal or contractual box — it brings clear competitive advantages:

  • Access to new markets: many chains only work with certified suppliers.
  • Brand protection: in a crisis, traceability lets you respond quickly and limit the damage.
  • Continuous improvement: audits force teams to review and optimize processes.
  • Consumer trust: customers know the product meets the highest levels of quality and safety.
  • Operational efficiency: documented systems reduce errors, duplication and costs.

Meeting food quality standards isn't a cost — it's an investment in sustainability and growth.

How to prepare to meet these standards

Preparing to meet food quality standards takes a combination of culture, process and technology.

  1. A culture of quality: every employee needs to understand why food safety matters and play their part in following procedures.
  2. Structured documentation: log issues, temperature checks, lab analyses and any other critical process.
  3. Ongoing training: keep teams up to date on regulations, risks and best practices.
  4. Internal audits: run periodic reviews to catch failures before an external auditor does.
  5. Digitizing records: replace paper and spreadsheets with digital systems that guarantee traceability and speed up audits.

Common challenges in implementation

Many companies run into difficulties when implementing standards:

  • Incomplete data: records that aren't captured in real time.
  • Resistance to change: staff who see the standards as bureaucracy.
  • Fragmented systems: issues logged in different departments with no central view.
  • Upfront cost: investing in technology and training can look expensive, even though the return is clear.

Overcoming these challenges takes leadership, strategic vision, and digital tools that simplify day-to-day operations.

Digitization and traceability: the key to successful audits
