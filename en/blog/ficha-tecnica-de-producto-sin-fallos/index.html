Flawless product spec sheet

The product specification sheet is one of the most critical documents in any company in the food industry. It summarizes the essential characteristics of a food product: composition, physicochemical, microbiological, sensory parameters, conditions of keepsstorage, shelf life and labeling requirements. However, its importance is not always reflected in the quality with which it is created or maintained. An incomplete, outdated or poorly managed food specification sheet can lead to serious non-conformities in audits, customer complaints or, in the worst case, a food safety issue. In this article we explain how to build it without errors and how to keep it in perfect order.

What a food product specification sheet should include

A good product specification is not a filler document. It is the technical reference used by production, quality, purchasing, logistics and customers to make decisions. That's why its content must be complete, accurate and verifiable. These are the sections that cannot be missing:

Product identification. Trade name, legal name, internal code, product family and document version. It seems basic, but it's common to find specification sheets without a version code or with names that don't match those in the ERP.

Description and composition. List of ingredients in descending order, allergen declaration (confirmed presence and possible cross-contamination), nutritional information and, where applicable, authorized nutrition or health claims.

Organoleptic characteristics. Description of the expected appearance, color, texture, smell and taste, with acceptable tolerance ranges. This section is key for quality control on receipt and dispatch.

Physicochemical parameters. pH, water activity (aw), moisture, salt content, Brix degrees or other relevant indicators depending on the type of product. Each parameter must state the target value and the minimum and maximum limits.

Microbiological criteria. Counts of pathogenic microorganisms and indicators, with the established limits and sampling frequency, in accordance with the Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005 on microbiological criteria for foodstuffs (Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005, 2005).

Shelf life and storage conditions. Expiry date or best-before date, storage temperature and humidity, conditions after opening and any special handling instructions.

Packaging and labeling. Type of packaging, material, format, net weight, batch marking and all mandatory information that must appear on the label.

Intended use and target consumer. The population the product is intended for, including whether it is indicated — or contraindicated — for vulnerable groups. This point connects directly with the hazard analysis of the HACCP system.

Common mistakes that fail audits

Los auditores de certificaciones como normas y estándares de calidad conocen muy bien los puntos débiles de las fichas técnicas. Estos son los fallos más repetidos:

Lack of version control. A specification sheet without a version number and approval date is uncontrollable. If the supplier or customer is working with an old version and the product has changed, the risk is obvious. Many non-conformities in audits come precisely from outdated versions still in circulation.

Incomplete or incorrect allergens. Omitting an allergen present in the formulation, or failing to correctly indicate the risk of cross-contamination, is one of the errors with the greatest legal and safety impact. The allergen declaration must be reviewed every time an ingredient or supplier changes.

Parameters without defined limits. Stating only the target value without specifying acceptance limits makes it impossible to make objective decisions during quality control. Every measurable parameter must have its tolerance range.

Outdated nutritional data. If the formulation or the supplier of an ingredient changes and the specification sheet is not updated, the nutritional information may be inaccurate. This affects both legal labeling and the reliability of the documentation for the customer.

Missing signature and approval. A specification sheet with no identified approval owner has no documentary validity. It must state who prepared it, who reviewed it and who approved it, together with the corresponding dates.

Not being aligned with the rest of the system. The spec sheet must be consistent with the product record in the ERP, with raw material purchasing specifications, and with quality control records. Inconsistencies between documents create confusion and non-conformities.

How to keep them updated and version-controlled

Having well-prepared spec sheets is only half the job. The real challenge is keeping them alive and under control. Here are some practical pointers:

Set up a clear approval workflow. Define who can modify a spec sheet, who reviews it, and who approves it before publishing. Without a documented workflow, changes get made informally and traceability is lost.

Link spec sheets to formulation or supplier changes. Every time a relevant change occurs — a new ingredient, a supplier change, a process modification — a review of the affected spec sheet should be triggered automatically. This link must be built into the change management procedure.

Control distribution. Knowing which version each customer or supplier has is as important as keeping the spec sheet updated internally. Keep a record of who received each version and when.

Set a mandatory periodic review. Even if nothing has changed, spec sheets must be reviewed at a minimum frequency — usually annual — to confirm they're still valid. This review must be logged.

Use a centralized system. Managing spec sheets in scattered spreadsheets or shared folders with no access control is a constant source of errors. A centralized system with version control, review alerts, and change traceability eliminates most of these problems.
