Root Cause Analysis: Methods and Examples

By Claudia · July 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Root cause analysis that prevents recurrence

When a non-conformity shows up at a food plant, the immediate reaction is usually to fix the visible problem: pull the batch, redo the process, correct the record. But without a rigorous root cause analysis, that same problem will resurface weeks or months later. Identifying why something happened — not just what happened — is the difference between managing quality reactively and building a genuinely robust system.

What root cause analysis is

Root cause analysis (RCA) is a structured methodology for looking past the symptom of a problem to identify its real origin. In the food industry, this approach is essential for meeting food safety management system requirements and for responding properly to internal and external audits.

It's not limited to major failures. Any deviation, however small it seems, can hide a systemic weakness: a poorly designed procedure, insufficient training, deteriorating infrastructure, or poor communication between departments. As set out in Regulation (EC) 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs (Regulation [EC] 852/2004, 2004), food business operators must implement permanent procedures based on HACCP principles — which requires exactly this kind of systematic analysis to guarantee control of critical points.

A well-executed root cause analysis produces three outcomes: the immediate correction to contain the problem, the corrective action to eliminate the cause, and the preventive action to stop similar situations from happening in other processes or areas.

Main methods: the 5 whys and Ishikawa

There are various tools for carrying out a root cause analysis. The most widely used in the food industry, for their practicality and ease of communication, are the 5 whys method and the Ishikawa diagram (or cause-and-effect diagram).

The 5 whys method involves repeatedly asking "why?" of each answer you get until you reach the root cause of the problem. Generally, three to five iterations are enough to move past the symptoms and reach a structural cause. For example:

  • Problem: Metal was detected in a batch of canned goods.
  • Why? The metal detector didn't trigger the alarm.
  • Why? The detector wasn't calibrated correctly.
  • Why? Calibration wasn't performed on the scheduled date.
  • Why? There was no alert system to notify the maintenance manager.
  • Why? The maintenance plan had no automatic reminders built in.

The root cause isn't the uncalibrated detector — it's the absence of a preventive maintenance management system. The corrective action needs to target that level.

The Ishikawa diagram, also known as the fishbone diagram, is especially useful when a problem has several possible causes or involves multiple departments. You start from the effect (the problem) and explore causes grouped into classic categories such as manpower, machinery, materials, method, environment, and measurement. This big-picture view makes teamwork easier and keeps the analysis from being skewed by a single department's perspective.

Both tools complement each other: the 5 whys help you dig deeper into each branch of the Ishikawa diagram, while the diagram makes sure no category of potential causes gets overlooked.

How to apply root cause analysis to real non-conformities

Non-conformity analysis is the most common context for applying RCA at a food company. The process follows a logical sequence that's worth respecting to get solid results.

1. Precise problem description. Before analyzing, you need to clearly define what happened: when, where, in which product, in which process, and how often. A vague description leads to a shallow analysis. Use objective data: records, test results, production reports.

2. Team formation. Root cause analysis shouldn't be a one-person job. Involving the people who took part in the process brings different perspectives and reduces the risk of organizational causes staying hidden. The quality manager acts as a facilitator, not the sole judge.

3. Applying the chosen tool. Pick the method that fits the problem's complexity. For specific, well-defined issues, the 5 whys are enough. For multi-factor or recurring problems, Ishikawa offers a more complete view.

4. Defining actions. Once the root cause is identified, corrective actions are set with an owner, a deadline, and verification criteria. It's essential to distinguish between the correction (what's done immediately) and the corrective action (what eliminates the cause).

5. Verifying effectiveness. No root cause analysis is complete without checking that the actions worked. After a reasonable amount of time, review whether the problem has reappeared and whether the related indicators have improved.

How to record the analysis and its closure in Solved

One of the main obstacles in non-conformity management is scattered information: the analysis sits in an email, the action in a spreadsheet, and the closure only in the manager's memory. This makes audits harder, blocks organizational learning, and makes it impossible to spot recurring patterns.

Root cause analysis, properly built into the system, is one of the pillars of that advantage.
