In many plants, inefficient incident assignment is one of the main quality management problems. Incidents that are poorly recorded or assigned to the wrong owner cause delays, wasted time, and additional costs that directly affect productivity. This article will cover what an automated workflow is, its benefits, how to design it effectively, practical examples, integration with other systems, and how tools like Solved simplify it for any type of plant.

What is an automated workflow in incident management

The incident workflows are processes designed to guide the path an incident follows from when it is recorded until it is resolved. In a manual workflow, an operator reports a problem and someone has to decide who to assign it to. This can cause delays or incorrect assignments.

An automated workflow, on the other hand, uses predefined rules and triggers to send the incident directly to the right person based on the type of problem, priority, shift, or role.

The basic elements of an automated workflow are:

  • Triggers: conditions that start the workflow (example: a quality incident is recorded on line X).
  • Roles: owners defined by area, shift, or hierarchical level.
  • Statuses: incident phases (recorded, in progress, resolved, closed).
  • Alerts and notifications: automatic alerts for owners and escalations in case of delay.

The difference with a manual workflow is clear: instead of relying on one person's interpretation, the system applies consistent, transparent rules to route each incident.

Benefits of workflow automation

Adopting incident workflows automated workflows brings immediate benefits for plants:

  • Faster assignment: each incident reaches the right owner without delay.
  • Fewer human errors: the usual confusion over who should take charge is eliminated.
  • Greater traceability and control: the system records every step, so the entire process can be audited.
  • Improved response times: by reducing assignment delays, problems are resolved sooner, minimizing their impact on production or quality.
  • Consistency in management: the same rules are always applied, avoiding subjective decisions.

An internal study of plants that have digitized their processes shows that resolution times can improve by up to 40% with well-configured automated workflows.

How to design an effective workflow

Designing incident workflows effective workflows requires planning and deep knowledge of plant processes. These are the main steps:

Define incident types and priorities

It is essential to categorize incidents: critical, major, minor, documentation-related, maintenance, quality, etc. Each type will have a different workflow and response times.

Assign owners based on role, shift or criticality

A flow needs clear rules: for example, a critical quality incident is assigned first to the supervisor and then to the quality manager. A minor incident, on the other hand, can go directly to the operator on shift.

Set up escalations and automatic notifications

If a critical incident isn't addressed within X minutes, the flow must escalate automatically to the next level up. This ensures problems never go unattended.

Build in validations and closures

The flow doesn't end when the owner marks the incident as resolved. It must include final validation by quality or production to ensure the fix is adequate.

Designing a flow isn't a theoretical exercise, but a living process that has to be reviewed and adjusted as incidents are detected day to day.

Let's look at two simple examples of incident workflows applied in plants:

  • Critical incident:
    Operator detects a serious defect in a batch → Record created in the system → Immediate notification to the line supervisor → Automatic escalation to the quality manager → Intervention by a specialized technician → Final validation → Closure with report and digital evidence.
  • Minor incident:
    Operator detects a minor deviation → Automatic record in the system → Assignment to the maintenance operator on shift → Resolution → Validation by supervisor → Closure.

These examples show how flows can adapt to different levels of criticality without creating confusion or delays.

Integration with other systems

A common mistake is thinking that incident workflows have to be duplicated in ERP, MES or GMAO. This creates unnecessary redundancy and complexity.

The ideal is for the incident management system to be autonomous but able to integrate with other systems to exchange relevant information:

  • ERP: link incidents to batches, orders or suppliers.
  • MES: associate incidents with specific production orders.
  • GMAO: carry incidents related to equipment over to generate maintenance orders.

This avoids duplicating records and ensures each area gets only the information it needs, keeping traceability in a single centralized system.

Common challenges and how to address them

Implementing automated incident workflows isn't without its challenges. Among the most common:

  • Ambiguity in roles: if owners aren't clearly defined, the flow can fail. Solution: map roles and responsibilities before automating.
  • Notification overload: too many alerts can overwhelm teams. Solution: define priority levels and only notify what's essential.
  • Errors in initial setup: poorly designed rules create bottlenecks. Solution: run pilot tests in specific areas before rolling out to the whole plant.

Overcoming these challenges means combining good planning with a flexible tool.
