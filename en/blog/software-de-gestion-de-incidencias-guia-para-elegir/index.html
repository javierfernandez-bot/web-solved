Issue Management Software: A Guide to Choosing the Right One

If you're considering making the move to issue management software, you probably already know something about your current process isn't quite working. Issues get lost, the people responsible aren't always in the loop, and when an audit comes around, reconstructing traceability turns into a race against the clock. This guide will help you understand what to look for in a solution, what signs indicate it's time to switch, and how a specialized tool can transform quality management at your food company.

What issue management software is and what it solves

An issue management software is a digital tool that centralizes the logging, tracking and resolution of all the deviations, non-conformities and quality problems that occur within an organization. Unlike a static document or spreadsheet, a specialized solution lets everyone involved work on the same information in real time, from anywhere, at any moment.

In the food industry, issues can crop up at any point in the chain: an out-of-spec raw material, a customer complaint, a packaging line failure or a deviation detected during an internal audit. What a good issue manager solves is exactly that: making sure no problem goes unrecorded, unowned or without a resolution date. Because an unmanaged issue doesn't disappear — it turns into a real risk to food safety and to your company's reputation.

Beyond one-off logging, this type of software gives you visibility into recurring patterns, makes it easier to open corrective actions, and lets you demonstrate to certification bodies that your management system runs in an orderly, documented way.

Signs that Excel is holding you back

For years, many quality departments have gotten by with shared spreadsheets, emails and network folders. It's an understandable approach when issue volume is low and the team is small. But there are clear signs that model has hit its limit.

The first sign is scattered information. If issues are spread across several Excel files, different email inboxes and maybe a WhatsApp group, it becomes impossible to get a real-time overall picture of what's happening. Everyone has their own version of the data, and coordination becomes a constant effort.

The second sign is lack of traceability. With a spreadsheet, it's hard to know who logged an issue, when it was assigned, what actions were taken and whether it was actually closed satisfactorily. If an external audit landed on you tomorrow, could you show the complete history of an issue from six months ago? In many cases, the honest answer is no.

The third sign is the absence of automatic alerts. With Excel, no one gets notified when an issue has been open too long or when an owner hasn't acted. Follow-up depends on someone remembering to check, which inevitably leads to things falling through the cracks and missed deadlines.

Key features: logging, assignment, alerts, KPIs and traceability

When evaluating an issue management program, it helps to be clear on the list of features that separate a genuinely useful tool from one that just digitizes the chaos. These are the ones you can't do without:

Fast, structured logging. The system should let any type of issue be logged in a few clicks, with standardized fields that guarantee the essential information is always captured: date, affected area, description, category and severity level. The easier it is to log, the more likely the team actually will.

Clear ownership assignment. Every issue should be assignable to a specific person, with a resolution deadline. That makes it clear who needs to act and by when, removing the ambiguity that lets problems go unresolved.

Alert and notification system. A good issue management app sends automatic notices when an issue is opened, when its deadline is approaching, or when a new comment is logged. This keeps the team informed without needing to constantly check the system.

Full lifecycle traceability. Every issue's history should always be available: who logged it, how it evolved, what actions were taken and who closed it. This traceability is essential for audits and certifications such as IFS, BRC or ISO 22000.

KPIs and quality reports. A good issue manager doesn't just store data — it turns it into useful information. Indicators like average resolution time, the most frequent categories, or the areas with the most issues let you make preventive decisions before a minor problem becomes a major one.

Links to corrective actions. Many issues require opening a corrective or preventive action. The software should let you create this link directly, keeping both records consistent and making joint follow-up easier.
