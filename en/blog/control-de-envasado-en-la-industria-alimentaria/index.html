Packaging Control in the Food Industry

By Claudia · September 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Packaging control in the food industry

Packaging control in the food industry is one of the most critical stages of the entire production chain and, paradoxically, one where the most silent errors pile up. A faulty seal, an incorrect label or an illegible batch code can look like minor issues when they happen, but their consequences reach much further: customer complaints, health alerts, or even product recalls. For quality managers, keeping packaging under control isn't optional — it's an operational and legal obligation.

Why packaging concentrates so many silent non-conformities: sealing, labeling and batch

Packaging lines run fast and continuously. That pace is their biggest production strength, but also their main source of quality risk. Problems aren't always visible to the naked eye and don't necessarily stop the line: a seal that intermittently loses its tightness, a roll of labels with an outdated allergen declaration, or a format change that doesn't update the batch code can run for hours before anyone catches it.

Labeling is by far where most packaging non-conformities pile up. Errors in the ingredient declaration, incorrect nutritional claims or missing mandatory information are violations that directly affect consumer safety and the product's legal compliance. As set out in Regulation (EC) 178/2002 (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are responsible for ensuring the food they place on the market meets food law requirements, which includes all the information on the packaging.

Sealing and airtightness are another risk area. A package that doesn't close properly compromises the product's shelf life, encourages microbiological contamination and can trigger a chain of complaints. And the batch — that small piece of data often printed on the fly at the end of the line — is the key piece of the entire traceability chain: if it's missing, illegible or wrongly assigned, your ability to respond to any issue is seriously limited.

The checks you can't skip on the line: weight, seal integrity, batch code and date

A robust packaging-line quality system must include, at a minimum, four types of systematic control. Skipping them or running them sporadically is an unnecessary risk that sooner or later turns into a bigger problem.

Weight control. Every product reference has a nominal weight and set tolerance limits. Continuously monitoring net weight — through in-line dynamic checkweighers or periodic manual checks — ensures the product matches what's declared and prevents both consumer shortchanging and losses from overfilling. All results must be logged with time, operator and product reference.

Seal integrity control. The seal test is especially critical for modified-atmosphere or vacuum-packed products, or those with a long shelf life. Both destructive and non-destructive methods exist, and the choice depends on the packaging type and the product's risk level. What matters is that the frequency and method are defined in the control plan, and that any deviation triggers an immediate alert.

Batch code and best-before or expiry date verification. This check should be done on the first units of every production run, after every stoppage, and after any shift or format change. Packaging traceability depends on this data being correct, complete and legible on 100% of the units produced.

Label review. Before starting each production run, you need to verify that the active label matches the reference about to be packaged, that it's the current version, and that all required fields are correct. A digital on-line checklist is the most effective way to make sure this check isn't skipped under production pressure.

What happens when packaging fails and no one logs it in time: recall, complaint, penalty

A lack of records in food packaging quality control doesn't make the problem go away — it just makes it harder to manage once it shows up. And packaging problems do show up. The question isn't if, but when and how far it spreads.

When a packaging defect reaches the consumer undetected on the plant floor, the consequences multiply. An isolated complaint can turn into a social-media alert; if an undeclared allergen is involved, the situation escalates into a health crisis requiring mandatory notification to the competent authorities. Recalling product from the market involves direct costs — logistics, destruction, communication — but above all reputational damage that's far harder to quantify and recover from.

From a regulatory standpoint, missing proper records also has consequences. Being unable to pinpoint the affected batch forces you to recall far larger quantities of product than would otherwise be strictly necessary. And if you can't demonstrate the checks performed during an official inspection, the company is left in a very vulnerable position facing possible administrative penalties.

Quality control in packaging isn't bureaucracy — it's the safety net that lets you respond quickly and precisely when something goes wrong. Without that net, any minor issue can turn into a major crisis.

How to digitize packaging control without slowing down the line

One of the most common arguments against rolling out systematic in-line checks is that they "slow down production." That's an understandable concern, but it disappears once the checks are properly digitized. The goal isn't to add steps for operators, but to replace paper and manual logging with agile tools that capture data the moment it's generated.

Quality software like Solved lets you configure digital checklists specific to each packaging line and product reference. The operator accesses it from a tablet or an on-line screen, logs checks in seconds, and the system automatically flags any value outside the set limits. No hunting for a notebook, no illegible handwriting, no data lost at the end of a shift.

Digitization also makes traceability far easier. By linking every control record to the batch code and production line, any later issue can be pinpointed in minutes instead of hours. This is exactly what Regulation (EC) 178/2002 (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002) requires when it comes to traceability obligations at every stage of the food chain.

What's more, data collected systematically generates indicators that help anticipate problems: if a given line or shift accumulates more weight or sealing deviations, that trend becomes visible on the dashboard before it turns into a bigger non-conformity. Packaging control doesn't have to be a burden on the line. With the right tools, it becomes a competitive advantage: fewer issues, less rework, fewer complaints, and more trust from customers and auditors. The technology is available — and so is the decision to put it to work.
