Complaint Management: A Step-by-Step Process

By Claudia · August 1, 2026 · 6 min read

Complaint management that builds customer loyalty

In the food industry, complaint management is much more than a mandatory administrative process: it's a real opportunity to demonstrate your company's commitment to quality and to turn a negative experience into a stronger bond with the customer. Companies that handle complaints with speed, transparency, and rigor don't just resolve individual problems — they build long-term trust. In this article, we explain how to structure an effective complaint-handling system and how Solved helps you manage it from start to finish.

Why a well-handled complaint retains customers

Receiving a complaint is never pleasant, but ignoring it or handling it poorly costs far more. Consumer behavior studies consistently show that a customer whose complaint is resolved satisfactorily is more loyal than a customer who never had a problem in the first place. Why? Because effective conflict resolution sends a clear message: the company listens, takes responsibility, and acts.

This matters even more in the food industry. Customer complaints can be related to product safety, foreign bodies, deviations in sensory characteristics, or labeling problems. Any of these cases can carry regulatory implications, but commercial ones too. A fast, well-documented response protects the company legally and, at the same time, shows the customer that their concerns are taken seriously.

What's more, Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 of the European Parliament and of the Council (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002) sets out the general principles of food law and underscores operators' responsibility for traceability and the management of food-safety-related issues. Having a formal complaints system isn't just good practice — in many contexts it's an implicit requirement of the European regulatory framework.

Ultimately, well-managed complaints strengthen brand image, reduce customer churn, and provide valuable input for improving production processes.

Logging, analysis, and response flow in complaint management

A robust complaint-handling system must cover at least three clearly defined phases: structured logging, root-cause analysis, and customer response. Improvising at any of these stages can lead to delays, inconsistent responses, and, in the worst case, a repeat of the same problem.

Structured logging: Every complaint must be documented from the outset with precise information: date received, customer details, affected product, batch, a detailed description of the problem, and the communication channel used. This initial record is the foundation the rest of the process is built on, and it lets you prioritize complaints based on severity or impact.

Root-cause analysis: Once a complaint is logged, the quality team needs to investigate the source of the problem. Tools like root-cause analysis (5 whys, Ishikawa diagram) help identify whether the failure originated on the production line, in storage, in transport, or in a raw material. This analysis doesn't just resolve the specific issue — it helps prevent it from happening again.

Customer response: The customer must receive a clear response, within a reasonable timeframe and with a level of detail appropriate to their profile. For industrial or distribution customers, the response typically includes a technical report on the corrective actions taken. In every case, communication should be honest and solution-oriented.

Setting up tracking indicators — such as average complaint closure time or recurrence rate — lets you assess how effective the process is and spot areas for continuous improvement.

Linking complaints to non-conformities and suppliers

One of the most common mistakes in complaint management is treating complaints as isolated events, disconnected from the rest of the quality system. In reality, a customer complaint is often the visible symptom of an internal non-conformity or a problem in the supply chain.

That's why complaint handling needs to be integrated with the issue and non-conformity management system. When the root cause of a complaint turns out to be a production defect, that non-conformity should be opened, investigated, and closed with documented corrective actions. That way, the complaint acts as the trigger for a real process improvement.

The link to suppliers is equally critical. If the cause of the problem is a defective raw material, unsuitable packaging, or an out-of-spec ingredient, you need to file a formal complaint with the supplier and, depending on severity, review their rating. A quality supplier audit gives you historical performance data on each supplier, which makes it easier to make informed decisions — from a formal warning to switching to a different approved supplier.

Standards such as BRCGS Global Standards (BRCGS, n.d.) require exactly this kind of traceability between complaints, non-conformities, and supplier actions, as part of quality management requirements. Having a digital system that connects all these pieces isn't a luxury — it's a necessity for any company that wants to keep its certifications and stay competitive.
