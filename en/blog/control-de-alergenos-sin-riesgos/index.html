Allergen Control: Requirements and How to Apply Them

By Claudia · July 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Allergen Control Without the Risks

Allergen control is one of the fundamental pillars of food safety at any company in the sector. Managing allergens properly isn't just a legal obligation — it's a direct responsibility toward consumer health. A serious allergic reaction can be fatal, and in most cases the root of the problem lies in gaps within the plant's own management system. In this article, we explain what you need to declare, what risks you need to control, and how a digital system like Solved can help you keep everything under control.

Which Allergens Must Be Declared, and Why

European regulation clearly and precisely sets out which substances must be declared on food labels. As stated in Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, laying down the general principles and requirements of food law (Regulation [EC] 178/2002, 2002), food business operators are responsible for ensuring that the food they sell is safe and correctly labeled, protecting consumers' right to accurate information.

The European Union currently recognizes 14 allergens that must be declared:

  • Cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats, and their hybrid strains)
  • Crustaceans
  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Peanuts
  • Soybeans
  • Milk (including lactose)
  • Tree nuts (almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, cashews, pecans, Brazil nuts, pistachios, and macadamia nuts)
  • Celery
  • Mustard
  • Sesame seeds
  • Sulphur dioxide and sulphites at concentrations above 10 mg/kg or 10 mg/l
  • Lupin
  • Molluscs

The obligation to declare these allergens applies to packaged food as well as food sold loose or served in catering. For quality managers in the food industry, knowing this list by heart is just the first step — managing it rigorously day to day is the real challenge.

Cross-Contamination Risks on the Plant Floor

One of the biggest challenges in food allergen control is cross-contamination. This happens when an allergen present in one product or ingredient unintentionally comes into contact with another product that shouldn't contain it. The most common routes for cross-contamination on the plant floor are:

  • Shared production lines: when products with and without allergens are made on the same equipment without proper separation measures.
  • Poorly cleaned utensils and equipment: product residue left on surfaces, pipes, pumps, or mixers can contaminate the next batch.
  • Staff handling: operators can transfer allergens through their hands, clothing, or footwear if they don't correctly follow hygiene protocols.
  • Poorly segregated raw materials: storing allergen ingredients next to allergen-free ones without the necessary physical barriers is a very common risk.
  • Changeover mistakes: during product changeovers on the line, without a clear verification protocol, it's easy for traces of the previous product to remain.

Cross-contamination isn't always visible to the naked eye, which makes it especially dangerous. Rigorous allergen management needs to account for both direct and indirect contamination routes, and establish preventive controls for each one.

Good Control and Cleaning Practices

To minimize the risk of cross-contamination and ensure accurate, reliable allergen labeling, quality teams need to implement a set of well-established good practices. These practices should be documented, reviewed periodically, and known by everyone involved in production.

The most effective measures include:

  • Allergen-specific risk analysis: integrated into the HACCP system, it should identify every critical point where contamination could occur and set control limits and corrective actions.
  • Physical and time separation: whenever possible, allergen-containing products should be made on dedicated lines. If that's not feasible, separate production shifts and validated cleaning procedures between them should be established.
  • Validated cleaning procedures: cleaning alone isn't enough — you have to prove it's effective at removing allergens. That means running surface tests (allergen swab tests) and documenting the results.
  • Ongoing staff training: every operator needs to know what allergens are, why they're dangerous, and the protocols for their work area. Training should be documented.
  • Reviewing technical data sheets and supplier specifications: any change in formulation or supplier can introduce new, undeclared allergens. Supplier management is therefore an essential part of allergen control.
  • Label control: before every product launch, verify that the label correctly reflects the actual composition, including "may contain traces of…" statements.

All of these measures are part of the requirements set out by the leading food safety certification standards. Schemes like BRCGS Global Standards (BRCGS, n.d.) dedicate specific chapters to allergen management, precisely because it's one of the most heavily audited areas and where non-conformities are most frequently found.
